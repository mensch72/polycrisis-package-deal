# Conditional pledges for a country platform: a two-page brief

*Draft for Jobst to edit and send. Research proposal; no government has asked for this.*

**The problem.** In a Just Energy Transition Partnership a recipient promises mitigation and reform, and
donors promise finance. Each side waits for the other. Donors pledge separately, through several
institutions, with no joint offer. When one donor leaves, as the United States did in 2025, nothing says
what the others then owe.

**The idea.** Each party files one public, conditional commitment, alone:

- the recipient: "we will reach level x, provided finance of at least y is allocated";
- each donor: "we will allocate z, provided the recipient is at level x and the other donors are at or
  above their listed allocations".

A fixed rule then computes the largest set of actions that satisfies everyone's conditions at once. That
is each party's liability. Nobody pays before the others' conditions are met, and a filing can be
withdrawn with notice. The mechanism and its equilibrium properties are in Heitzig (2025),
[doi:10.3390/g16060058](https://doi.org/10.3390/g16060058).

**What is new for practice.**

1. Conditions refer to a public register, so they can be checked.
2. Filings can be stepped: a first deal among some donors, a larger one when the rest join. If a late
   joiner leaves, the outcome falls back to the first step and not to zero.
3. Before anyone files, the method tests whether a proposed package is stable: whether any party or group
   would do better by leaving or by undercutting.

**A worked skeleton.** We built a small model in the shape of the South African partnership: South Africa,
Germany, the United Kingdom, the EU with the EIB, and France. Pledge sizes are from the partnership's own
register of 30 June 2026. All preferences and costs are invented placeholders. In this skeleton a stable
two-step package exists: a first step among four parties with about USD 3.6 billion allocated, and a second
step with all five and about USD 7.1 billion. It also shows a design point that does not depend on our
numbers: if every donor caps its share of the total, a first step with only two donors cannot be acceptable
to both.

**What we would like from you.** Thirty minutes, on three questions:

1. Which of our placeholders are furthest from reality? Two matter most: how a recipient values a dollar of
   grant-equivalent finance, and what a tier of ambition costs it.
2. Could a finance ministry or a development bank sign an allocation schedule that depends on other donors'
   allocations? Under what instrument?
3. Who has tried something like this, and why did it fail or succeed?
