# From pause-willingness to a pause: conditional commitments for entry

*Two-page outline for Wes Holliday and Will Fithian. Draft for Jobst to edit and send.*
*Disclosure: drafted with Claude, an AI system built by Anthropic, a company the proposal concerns.*

**The gap we would address.** Your report shows that a hardwired pause could be verified once agreed. It
defines pause-willingness so that each leader has some terms under which they would pause if confident the
others pause under those terms, and it notes that this "does not assume that there is a single set of
terms" acceptable to all. Section 2.3 shows that cooperation can be an equilibrium. Three steps before
that are open: whether common terms exist, how the parties reach them without anyone moving first, and
which groups could upset them.

**The tool.** In the mechanism of Heitzig (2025, doi:10.3390/g16060058) each party files, alone, a
commitment that is a function of the others' commitments. A fixed rule returns the largest set of actions
that satisfies every filed condition. For public-good problems its strong equilibria are known. We are
extending it to cases where some actions harm some parties, by staging entry: a small coalition first,
others joining on top, each stage tested for stability against every group.

**Why it fits your design.**

1. Your one reciprocal mechanism is already of this form: states "could agree to produce some number of
   chips conditional on others doing the same".
2. You expect entry by informal agreement, with a treaty later. Unilateral filings need no treaty.
3. Your ledger gives levers in common units, and allowances that start high and shrink are a ladder.
4. The binding constraints are asymmetric. The United States holds the breakout lever, China the census lever.

**What your numbers already imply.** Reading durability off your ledger at α = 0.25 and 57 % a year, with all
else in order (our arithmetic on your illustrative values; please check):

| Years of durability | China conceals | cooperates a little | cooperates fully |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States keeps its whole fleet at home | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.5 |
| keeps half | 1.0 | 2.0 | 2.0 |
| keeps a tenth | 1.0 | 5.6 | 5.6 |
| keeps a residual | 1.0 | 5.8 | 10.2 |

Neither side can buy durability alone. A pair of conditional filings, fleet moved out against census
cooperation, is the natural object.

**A first skeleton.** Three actors: the United States, China, and the chokepoint jurisdictions as a bloc.
Your ledger, thresholds, stocks and census scenarios; invented preferences. By full enumeration:

- terms acceptable to all and stable against every group exist in the skeleton;
- they can be reached in your order: the two lead states first, the suppliers on top;
- if the suppliers leave, the outcome falls back to the first stage; if a lead state leaves, to nothing;
- the filings stay an equilibrium when the temptation to train on alone is tripled, which turns the stage
  game from a stag hunt into a Prisoner's Dilemma. Conditional filings need to bind, not to be benign.

The preferences are placeholders, so these are properties of a format, not findings.

**What a companion analysis could deliver.**

1. A test, on your ledger, of whether common terms exist for stated ranges of preferences.
2. Entry paths: which first coalitions can start, and what each later entrant must be offered.
3. Graded entry: filings on the allowance schedule, shrinking as the other side's census advances.
4. The role of strategic reserves next to conditional filings: insurance twice, or substitutes?
5. What a filing can condition on when a level, such as hidden chips, is only an estimate.
6. A response rule for a state's breakout, which the report leaves to the parties.

**What we would need from you.** Your judgement on plausible preference orderings for the lead states and
the suppliers; a check of how we read the ledger; and, if it appeals, a slot in a tabletop exercise.

**What this is not.** A view on whether a pause is desirable or likely.
